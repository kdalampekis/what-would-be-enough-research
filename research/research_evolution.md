# How the research question developed

This document answers one question: how did the project get from "can LLMs recognise uncertainty?" to Minimal Sufficient Epistemic Planning over information and computation, and how did the literature and the project's own results change the question along the way? It also explains why detection, resource diagnosis, action targeting, sufficiency, minimality and stopping are measured separately. The initial idea was not the same as the final one; Sections 5 to 7 show what changed and why. The last section states where the evidence stands, in agreement with [`../paper/report.md`](../paper/report.md), which is the authoritative statement of the final claims. Reference numbers follow [`literature_timeline.md`](literature_timeline.md), Section 15. Every result carries its evidence tier.

## 1. Starting point: recognising uncertainty

The first LLM-era question was whether a model can tell how confident it should be. The literature answered with calibration studies and uncertainty estimates [12]–[14] ([`working_documents/LITERATURE_TIMELINE.md`](working_documents/LITERATURE_TIMELINE.md) §2). The author's earlier project, STML, worked in this area: it contains selective-prediction and abstention metrics, and its recorded findings are that deeper logical problems can lower accuracy while confidence stays high, and that certificate-style checks can outperform raw confidence ([`working_documents/PRIOR_WORK.md`](working_documents/PRIOR_WORK.md) §1). Those findings are background and are not results of this project.

A confidence value does not say what to do. The working timeline draws this lesson from the abstention literature: uncertainty estimation and decision policy are different problems (timeline §4; the illustration there is the timeline's own).

## 2. From detection to diagnosis

The next question is why the model is uncertain. ConfuseBench [19] asks whether uncertainty comes from missing documents, limited capability or an ambiguous query. Work on adaptive retrieval and clarification asks whether the model knows that it needs external information (timeline §5). The working documents also describe one paper, EPRAG [26]†, as putting diagnosis of the evidence state before the choice of action (timeline §11); that description was not confirmed at the source.

For this project the lesson was that a failure to notice a gap must not be confused with a failure to say what kind of gap it is. The problem specification therefore lists detection and diagnosis as separate stages ([`working_documents/PROBLEM_SPEC.md`](working_documents/PROBLEM_SPEC.md) §1).

## 3. From diagnosis to the choice of intervention

Once a gap is diagnosed, the agent must choose an intervention. The 2026 literature studies this as a choice of action type: act, ask or relax a constraint [2]; ask, look up, act or commit [3]; query or simulate [6]; retrieve or think [27]. Two findings shaped this project. A correct action type is not a usable intervention: in [2], all Relax labels were correct and no response passed the repair check. And the same uncertainty signal can mean that extra computation helps or hurts [4], so "needs computation" and "computation will help" are different things (timeline §7, §9).

The simplest version of the project's question would be a binary choice between asking and computing. The problem specification rejects that as the final question: "ASK vs COMPUTE is only a controlled diagnostic slice" (`PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §9). The repository does not record a period in which the binary version was the main question. Not recorded. That the specification has a section against it suggests it was considered and set aside (retrospective interpretation).

## 4. From the choice of intervention to minimal sufficient combinations

Two strands moved the question from "which type of action" to "which set of actions is enough".

- MT-InfoSeek [1] asks which variables make the answer unique and defines a minimal sufficient set of queries. The working timeline's lesson: needing information is not the same as knowing what information is enough (timeline §6).
- Work on tools and evidence separates relevant from sufficient [5], [23]. The right action is not the most relevant one (timeline §8).

Each strand covers one resource. The combination was not found: one action space with information and computation, states that need either, both or neither, and an exact answer for the cheapest sufficient plan (timeline §13; [`working_documents/NOVELTY_MAP.md`](working_documents/NOVELTY_MAP.md) §2). This gave the research question:

> When an AI agent lacks sufficient grounds for a reliable decision, can it determine whether the minimal sufficient epistemic plan requires additional information, additional computation, both, or neither — and can it construct that plan correctly? (`PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §1)

and the name of the task, Minimal Sufficient Epistemic Planning (MSEP).

## 5. What changed while the task was formalised (2026-11-03 and 2026-11-04, before any model was called)

| Change | Before | After | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning of a computation action | A computation "transforms or verifies information already available"; the first families used simple task kinds | Revised on 2026-11-03: a certified computation or verification whose result is not yet part of the agent's decision state; tasks chosen so that doing them mentally is not the dominant shortcut | `PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §5, §5.1; `PRIOR_WORK.md` §8 |
| Sufficiency criterion | Deterministic and probabilistic criteria both described | Decided on 2026-11-03: the core benchmark uses deterministic logical sufficiency only; the posterior-threshold rule is kept apart, because it is not monotone in information and the obligation properties do not hold for it | `PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §6 |
| Form of a plan | "An ordered sequence or conditional policy"; the working timeline's example of a gold label is ask, then verify, then stop | The benchmark scores static plans: a set of actions chosen before any result is seen. Conditional plans (Level 4) were specified and not implemented | `PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §7, §12; timeline §16; [`../paper/extended_report.md`](../paper/extended_report.md), Appendix D |
| What an obligation is | A named unresolved requirement, such as "truth(A) unresolved" | A minimal set of unknowns on which a Q-true and a Q-false possibility differ. A plan is sufficient exactly when it reveals something in every obligation, so the cheapest plan is a minimum-cost hitting set (proved) | `PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §8; extended report, Appendix D |
| Benchmark text | First template | An adversarial audit showed the first template could be solved by reading one rule. It was rebuilt, and text-only heuristics are reported beside the models | `paper/report.md` §3.2 |
| A cited finding | "Models ask the wrong variables" | Corrected on 2026-11-03: the abstract of [1] says a wrong query order lowers accuracy | `NOVELTY_MAP.md` §9 |

## 6. Why six things are measured separately

The specification names five stages (detection, diagnosis, planning or targeting, sufficiency, control or stopping) and says that the scientific target is the later stages while detection is measured apart (`PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §1). It also lists the failures the benchmark must tell apart: the right class with the wrong action; a relevant but insufficient action; a sufficient but non-minimal plan; premature stopping; unnecessary actions after sufficiency (§9). The methods of the report implement this with three modes and with separate scores for sufficiency, optimality and stopping (`paper/report.md` §3.3).

| Component | Question | How it is measured | Why it is kept apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detection | Does the model notice that the state is not sufficient? | End-to-End mode: the model sees only the state | Uncertainty detection is an existing, separate problem. If it is not controlled, a detection failure looks like a planning failure. Detection-Controlled mode tells the model that the state is insufficient and removes this confound (`PROBLEM_SPEC.md` §10; `NOVELTY_MAP.md` §6) |
| Resource diagnosis | Which class of resource is needed? | Class accuracy in Detection-Controlled mode | A model could name the class and still choose a wrong plan, or the reverse. On the held-out split the registered hypothesis that diagnosis is easier than planning was supported for no model, and for two models the difference was significant in the other direction (registered held-out; `paper/report.md` §4.1). Without a separate score this would not be visible |
| Action targeting | Which concrete actions? | Diagnosis-Controlled mode: the class is given, the plan is scored | It isolates plan construction from detection and diagnosis. If a model fails End-to-End and succeeds here, the problem is earlier in the chain (`NOVELTY_MAP.md` §6) |
| Sufficiency | Would the plan settle the question whatever its results are? | The oracle checks that every outcome of the plan leaves the target decided | A relevant action need not be sufficient. Sufficiency alone is also a weak score here: the strongest text-only heuristic gives a sufficient plan on 0.85 of resolvable development states (no-model baseline; extended report §3.2) |
| Minimality | Is the plan a cheapest sufficient plan? | Optimal means sufficient at the oracle's minimum cost; every tied cheapest plan counts | A model can be sufficient and wasteful. In follow-up Study 2 gemma's plans are sufficient on 0.89 of problems and cheapest on 0.31 and 0.16 (follow-up study, registered after the held-out results were viewed; [`../results/aggregate/followup/followup_report.md`](../results/aggregate/followup/followup_report.md)) |
| Stopping | Should the model act at all? | An empty plan with the right answer on already-sufficient states; deferral on states that nothing can resolve | The right plan is empty in two different situations, and a score for found plans does not see either. The controlled modes cannot be used on already-sufficient states, because their prompts say the state is insufficient, so stopping is measured End-to-End ([`../PREREG_T1B.md`](../PREREG_T1B.md)) |

The separation also limits what can be concluded. On the held-out split no item of the three stronger models fails in all three modes, so none of their errors is attributed to planning itself (registered as descriptive, [`../PREREG_FINAL.md`](../PREREG_FINAL.md) §5; `paper/report.md` §4.1).

## 7. How the results refined the question (during the sprint)

**7.1 The expected planning failures did not appear for strong models.** The plan of 2026-11-03 expected that substantial planning error would remain once detection was controlled, that sufficiency would be harder than relevance, and that states needing both resources would be a major failure regime ([`working_documents/EXPERIMENT_PLAN.md`](working_documents/EXPERIMENT_PLAN.md) §2). The first diagnostic run on 10 development families (2026-11-13; diagnostic, not confirmatory) showed three models at ceiling on sufficiency and optimality. Another model, gemma, lost optimality to redundancy: sufficient plans padded with unnecessary actions, also when the class was given, so the failure was one of minimality and not of diagnosis ([`../results/aggregate/diagnostics_2026-11-13/FINDINGS.md`](../results/aggregate/diagnostics_2026-11-13/FINDINGS.md)).

**7.2 The literature pointed to consistency under one edit and to stopping.** On the same day six papers were read at the source ([`working_documents/LITERATURE_FAILURE_MAP.md`](working_documents/LITERATURE_FAILURE_MAP.md)). Following the matched pairs of [2], a challenge tier was designed in which one edit to a state changes the cheapest plan ([`working_documents/CHALLENGE_V4_DESIGN.md`](working_documents/CHALLENGE_V4_DESIGN.md)). The design document discloses that this tier was designed after seeing which models were at ceiling. Its pilot was inconclusive under its registered rules (registered development). An exploratory observation in the pilot was that one strong model kept acting on states that an edit had just made sufficient ([`../results/aggregate/t1_pilot_dev/T1_PILOT_FINDINGS.md`](../results/aggregate/t1_pilot_dev/T1_PILOT_FINDINGS.md)). This became a registered hypothesis on fresh development families and was supported for two of four models (registered development; `PREREG_T1B.md`; [`../docs/EXPERIMENT_INDEX.md`](../docs/EXPERIMENT_INDEX.md) §3). At this point the question had narrowed from "can models build the plan?" to "do they know when the plan is empty?".

**7.3 The mitigation turned into a question about extraction.** The Epistemic Obligation Planner was registered as: the model extracts the formal state, an exact planner chooses the actions. As registered it failed for three of four models on the development split, because the registered prompt never showed the layout the reader requires (registered development; `paper/report.md`, Table B1). Amendment 1 added the layout; the amended method is development-informed. A parser with no model, written as the inverse of the text generator, also solved the benchmark's own wording (no-model baseline). So a high score for the mitigation on that wording does not show that the model contributes anything. A rewording study was registered separately to test this (`paper/report.md` §3.4, §3.5).

**7.4 The held-out split confirmed strong planning and did not support most hypotheses.** On 63 held-out families, three models give a cheapest sufficient plan on 0.93, 0.98 and 0.98 of resolvable states and gemma on 0.42 (Detection-Controlled; on the development split, descriptively: 0.96, 0.98, 0.96 and 0.45). The hypothesis that diagnosis is easier than planning was supported for no model; that relevance is confused with sufficiency, for one model with a small effect; that states needing both resources are harder, for none (registered held-out; `paper/report.md` §4.1; [`../results/aggregate/final_test/RESULTS_TABLES.md`](../results/aggregate/final_test/RESULTS_TABLES.md)). The amended mitigation reached 0.99–1.00, and so did the parser (prospective test of an amended method; no-model baseline). On the 169 states that one edit had made sufficient, qwen3.5-397b still proposed an action on 0.20 [0.14, 0.27] and gemma on 0.72 [0.64, 0.79] (registered held-out). The held-out results were first viewed during a provider outage, before one model's run had finished, under a rule committed beforehand; the final numbers are from the complete data ([`../results/aggregate/final_test/INTERIM_NOTE.md`](../results/aggregate/final_test/INTERIM_NOTE.md)).

At this stage the project's reading was that the gap is in stopping, not in planning. The earlier long report says so in these words (`paper/extended_report.md` §5).

**7.5 A follow-up study showed that part of the stopping finding was an artifact of the benchmark's wording.** The benchmark text says of every computational fact that its value "is not yet known", while the line of known values can list it. An exploratory split showed that 21 of the strong model's 34 unnecessary plans only re-obtain values that the text lists as known (exploratory; `paper/report.md` §4.2). Follow-up Study 1 tested this on 60 fresh train families, with each state rendered in the original wording and in a wording that says a computational value is unknown unless it is listed as known. With the clarified wording qwen3.5-397b acts on 0.03 [0.01, 0.05] of already-sufficient states instead of 0.14, and gemma on 0.59 [0.51, 0.66] instead of 0.73 (follow-up study, registered after the held-out results were viewed; [`../PREREG_FOLLOWUP.md`](../PREREG_FOLLOWUP.md); `followup_report.md`). The registered held-out result stands as measured. Its interpretation changed: for the strong models most of the over-intervention was induced by the benchmark's wording. What remains is gemma's failure, large under either wording, and imperfect deferral by the strong models (`paper/report.md` §4.2, §5). The final report replaced the claim "what remains is stopping" with this narrower one ([`../paper/extended_report.md`](../paper/extended_report.md), note at the top, which marks that claim as superseded).

**7.6 Two more follow-up studies completed the picture.** Study 2 ran the cost-sensitivity experiment of the original plan (E10): the two strong models tested give the cheapest plan on 0.95–0.98 of problems on both sides of a price boundary; gemma on 0.31 above and 0.16 below (follow-up study, registered after the held-out results were viewed). Study 3 asked whether rejected extractions can be repaired with one more call: gemma's exact-state rate rose from 0.21 to 0.78 and its accepted-but-wrong extractions from 0.07 to 0.16; deepseek's exact-state rate rose from 0.89 to 0.97 with no accepted-but-wrong extraction (exploratory follow-up; [`../results/aggregate/repair/repair_report.md`](../results/aggregate/repair/repair_report.md)).

**7.7 The rewording study located the open problem.** On four rewordings of 137 development states the parser scores 0.00 on three. The model extractor holds for the two qwen models and breaks for deepseek (0.81 on the syntax wording) and gemma (0.05) (rewording study, registered separately; [`../results/aggregate/robust_dev/robust_report.md`](../results/aggregate/robust_dev/robust_report.md)). The final statement of the project is therefore about a different step than the one the plan started from: "Planning on a correct formal state is reliable; obtaining that state from language is the open problem." (`paper/report.md`, abstract).

## 8. The initial idea and the final one

| | As first written (2026-11-03 to 2026-11-04) | As it stands in the final report |
|---|---|---|
| Expected main finding | Substantial planning error; confusion of relevance with sufficiency; mixed states as a major failure regime (`EXPERIMENT_PLAN.md` §2) | Three of four models plan near ceiling; most registered hypotheses not supported |
| Where the weakness lies | In constructing the plan | For the weak model, in minimality, cost and stopping, not in sufficiency. For the mitigation, in translating language into a formal state |
| Stopping | Two of six expected findings: stopping too early, and acting after the decision is justified (timeline §17) | First the main apparent weakness of a strong model; then shown to be mostly an artifact of the benchmark text for that model, and real for the weak model |
| Mitigation | Expected to reduce insufficient actions, premature stopping and excess cost against confidence-based and routing baselines (`EXPERIMENT_PLAN.md` §2, H6) | Works where extraction works; a parser with no model does as well on regular text; the confidence-based and routing baselines were not built (`docs/EXPERIMENT_INDEX.md` §3, notes) |
| Form of a plan | Ordered or conditional | Static set of actions |
| Next research step | Not recorded | Faithful translation from natural language to a formal state ([`future_research.md`](future_research.md)) |

## 9. Where the evidence stands

The statements below match `paper/report.md`. Each is followed by its tier.

- **Planning.** On the held-out split three of four open-weight models find a cheapest sufficient plan on 0.93–0.98 of resolvable states and the fourth on 0.42. *Registered held-out.* The development split gave 0.96, 0.98, 0.96 and 0.45. *Development split, descriptive: no test was registered for these rates on that split.*
- **Registered hypotheses.** Diagnosis easier than planning: supported for no model, reversed for two. Relevance confused with sufficiency: supported for qwen3.8-27b only, with a small effect. Mixed states harder: not supported. *Registered held-out.*
- **Prices.** The two strong models tested follow the cheapest plan across a price boundary; gemma does not. *Follow-up study, registered after the held-out results were viewed.*
- **Stopping.** On newly sufficient held-out states qwen3.5-397b acts on 0.20 [0.14, 0.27] and gemma on 0.72; the other two on 0.00. When nothing available can settle the question, the three stronger models defer on 0.89, 0.87 and 0.97 of states and gemma never does. *Registered held-out.* With clarified wording on fresh families, acting on already-sufficient states falls from 0.14 to 0.03 for qwen3.5-397b and from 0.73 to 0.59 for gemma; so most of the strong model's over-intervention came from an ambiguity in the benchmark text, and the weak model's stopping failure is real. *Follow-up study, registered after the held-out results were viewed.*
- **Mitigation.** The Epistemic Obligation Planner reaches 0.99–1.00 on held-out data. *Prospective test of an amended method; as registered it failed for three of four models on the development split.* A parser with no model also reaches 1.00. *No-model baseline.*
- **Rewording.** The parser fails on three of four rewordings. The model extractor holds for the two qwen models and breaks for deepseek and gemma. *Rewording study, registered separately.*
- **Repair.** One repair call recovers most rejected extractions and adds some accepted-but-wrong ones for gemma. *Exploratory follow-up.*
- **Scope.** The tasks are small synthetic logic problems in generated language; four open-weight models from one provider, temperature 0, one answer per item; static plans with guaranteed sufficiency. The results are not evidence about open-ended decisions (`paper/report.md` §5, Appendix A).
- **Conclusion of the report.** Planning on a correct formal state is reliable. Obtaining that state from language is the open problem.
